# Rhetorical Situation Snapshot

## Student handout

### Why you are doing this

Strong rhetorical analysis explains not only what a writer says, but how the
writer's choices make the message work for a particular audience and purpose.

### Your target

You will analyze how a writer uses specific rhetorical choices to shape an
argument for a particular audience and purpose.

### The task

Choose one text from the teacher-provided set. You will create a short analysis
that explains how three rhetorical choices help the writer accomplish the text's
purpose.

### What to do

1. Annotate the text. Mark the speaker, audience, occasion, purpose, and central
   claim. Label at least three meaningful choices, such as diction, syntax,
   imagery, examples, contrast, tone, or structure.
2. Complete the evidence check below for each choice.
3. Write a 350–500 word analysis. Your central claim should name the writer's
   purpose and explain how the choices work together. For each choice, include a
   short quotation or precise reference, explain its effect on the audience, and
   connect that effect to the writer's purpose.
4. Add a two-sentence reflection: Which choice was most important to the
   argument, and what made it more effective than the others?

### Submit

- Your annotated text
- The completed evidence check
- Your 350–500 word analysis
- Your two-sentence reflection

Submit all four pieces as one document. Use paragraph numbers or line numbers
when they are available. You may write by hand for the annotation and upload a
clear photo if your teacher approves.

### Success criteria

Your analysis is successful when it:

- makes a defensible claim about the writer's purpose and rhetorical situation;
- selects three meaningful choices rather than merely naming devices;
- uses precise evidence from the assigned text;
- explains how each choice affects the audience;
- connects each explanation to the writer's purpose; and
- stays analytical instead of retelling or summarizing the text.

### Support

You may use these sentence frames while drafting:

- By using **[choice]** in **[specific passage]**, the writer **[verb]** the
  audience's **[belief, emotion, attention, or expectation]**, which helps the
  writer **[purpose]**.
- This choice matters because **[explain the connection, not just the effect]**.
- Although **[alternate reading or possible weakness]**, the choice ultimately
  **[judgment about effectiveness]** because **[reason]**.

### AI-use note

You may use AI to clarify the directions, define a rhetorical term, or help you
generate questions about your own draft. Do not use AI to write the analysis or
choose the evidence for you. If you use AI, include the tool name and one sentence
describing how you used it. Your annotations, evidence check, and reflection should
show your own thinking.

## Teacher notes

### Design rationale

- **Mode:** low-stakes analytical practice.
- **Learning target:** students explain how rhetorical choices shape an argument
  for an audience and purpose.
- **Evidence:** annotated text, evidence table, short analysis, and reflection.
- **Recommended context:** one teacher-selected editorial, speech excerpt, or
  public-facing argument of approximately 500–900 words.
- **Time:** one 50-minute class for annotation and evidence collection; 25–35
  minutes for drafting or revision.
- **Alignment:** specific standards should be attached only after the teacher
  selects the local course framework and text.

### Single-point rubric

| Criterion | Evidence of success | Notes for feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhetorical situation | The response identifies a plausible audience, purpose, and claim. |  |
| Analysis | The response explains how choices shape audience response and support purpose. |  |
| Evidence | The response uses precise, relevant textual evidence for all three choices. |  |
| Reasoning | The response connects evidence, effect, and purpose instead of listing devices. |  |
| Clarity | The response is organized, readable, and within the requested scope. |  |

### Differentiation options

- Provide a text with paragraph numbers and a short glossary of rhetorical terms.
- Preselect two possible choices for students who need a narrower entry point, while
  requiring students to explain the effect independently.
- Allow speech-to-text or an audio explanation paired with the evidence table when
  the learning target remains visible.
- Extend the task by asking students to compare how a second text addresses a
  similar audience or purpose.

### Revision signal

If students name devices without explaining effects, model one evidence-to-effect-
purpose chain before assigning another paragraph. If students summarize instead of
analyzing, have them underline every sentence that names a choice and add one sentence
for the audience effect and one for the purpose connection.
