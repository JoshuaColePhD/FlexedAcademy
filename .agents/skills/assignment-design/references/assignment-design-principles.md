# Assignment-design principles

This reference supports the `assignment-design` skill. Read it when choosing among
assignment types, building a rubric, or checking a complex assignment for alignment.

## The alignment chain

Keep this chain visible during design:

`learning target → student evidence → task/process → criteria → feedback`

If one link changes, inspect the links after it. For example, changing an argument
essay into a podcast should change the evidence and criteria, not just the title.

## Assignment modes

| Mode | Best for | Design emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practice task | First attempt or low-stakes rehearsal | One skill, short completion time, fast feedback |
| Reading or source response | Comprehension plus interpretation | Specific evidence, analytical move, short response |
| Performance or product | Transfer and authentic application | Audience, constraints, quality criteria, revision |
| Project sequence | Complex work over several days or weeks | Milestones, drafts, feedback loops, manageable load |
| Collaborative task | Discourse, problem solving, or co-construction | Individual accountability, roles, group product |
| Peer review | Revision and metacognition | Model the feedback process and tie prompts to criteria |

Do not default to the largest artifact. A small task that reveals the target skill is
often more useful than a polished project with weak evidence.

## Student-facing prompt blueprint

Write the student version in this order when the assignment needs a full handout:

1. **Purpose:** what this work helps students practice or understand.
2. **Target:** what students should be able to do by the end.
3. **The task:** the central question, problem, or performance.
4. **Process:** numbered steps, including where students should pause, check, or revise.
5. **Requirements:** length, evidence, format, collaboration, materials, and constraints.
6. **What to submit:** name every component and where it goes.
7. **How it will be judged:** plain-language criteria linked to the target.
8. **Support:** model, checklist, vocabulary, planning tool, conference, or alternative
   access route.

Use concrete verbs and examples. Replace “write about the reading” with an analytical
move such as “compare how the two authors use evidence to frame the same issue.” State
what counts as evidence and distinguish summary from analysis when that distinction
matters.

## Rubric selection

Use a completion checklist when the work is practice and the teacher mainly needs to
see whether students attempted the target. Use a single-point rubric when the teacher
wants concise feedback against a few criteria. Use an analytic rubric when:

- the assignment is consequential;
- multiple people will score it;
- students need detailed revision guidance; or
- the product has genuinely separable dimensions.

Each criterion should name an observable quality, not a personality judgment. Levels
should describe the work, not rank the student. Keep the rubric shorter than the
assignment's cognitive work requires; criteria should clarify priorities, not create
an essay about grading.

## Scaffolding without lowering rigor

Choose support based on the barrier:

- **Task comprehension:** rewrite the directions, add a worked example, or show the
  first step.
- **Language demand:** define essential terms, provide sentence frames, or permit
  speech-to-text.
- **Planning:** add a checklist, organizer, milestone, or brief conference.
- **Prior knowledge:** provide a compact refresher or prerequisite practice.
- **Executive load:** chunk the task and make the submission components explicit.

Do not pre-write the analysis, remove the key decision, or turn a transfer task into a
recall worksheet merely because the first draft is difficult. Offer an alternate mode
of expression only when it still demonstrates the target.

## Academic integrity by design

When AI use matters, make the policy specific: allowed, limited, or prohibited uses;
what students must disclose; and what evidence of process is expected. Prefer designs
that naturally require course-specific evidence, personal observation, drafts,
conferences, oral explanation, or reflection when those are instructionally justified.
Do not promise that a task is “AI-proof,” and do not add surveillance requirements
without a clear learning purpose.

## Quality gate

Before delivery, check:

- Could a student explain what to do after reading the first page?
- Is the requested work evidence of the stated target?
- Is the workload plausible for the available time and resources?
- Are required texts, data, or standards actually available and accurately represented?
- Are success criteria observable and prioritized?
- Does each scaffold improve access without doing the thinking for the student?
- Are student instructions free of teacher-only notes and private information?
- Would the teacher know what to revise if the first student submissions reveal a gap?
